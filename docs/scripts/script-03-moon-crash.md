## Script

**\[Hook\]**

Look up on a clear night and the Moon seems to hang there, perfectly still. But it is moving very fast, all the time. So here is a strange question. What would happen if the Moon came crashing down?

The short answer is that it cannot happen. The long answer teaches us a lot about the Moon, our tides, and how moons are born and die. So let us walk through it, one step at a time, and keep fact and imagination clearly apart.

**\[Why the Moon does not fall\]**

First, what we know. The Moon is on average about three hundred and eighty four thousand kilometres away. It has a little over one percent of the Earth's mass.

Earth's gravity pulls on it constantly. In a sense, the Moon is always falling toward us. But it is also moving sideways, at about one kilometre every second. It keeps falling, and it keeps missing. That endless missing is what we call an orbit.

To fall straight down, the Moon would have to lose most of that sideways speed. No known natural process could take it away.

**\[What is really happening\]**

In fact, the opposite is happening. The Moon is slowly drifting away.

During the Apollo missions, astronauts left special mirrors on the Moon's surface. Since then, observatories on Earth have fired lasers at them and timed the reflections. Those measurements show the Moon moving away by about three point eight centimetres every year. That is roughly as fast as your fingernails grow.

The cause is the tides. The Moon's gravity raises the oceans, and the Earth's spin drags those bulges slightly ahead. They tug the Moon forward, and very slowly it rises into a wider orbit.

**\[Imagine: the Moon comes closer\]**

Now, the thought experiment. Imagine, purely as an exercise, that some giant hand slowly pushed the Moon toward us. This part is imagination, guided by physics.

The first thing we would notice is the tides. Tidal pull grows very quickly as the distance shrinks. Halve the distance, and the tidal stretching becomes about eight times stronger.

High tides would rise far higher than today. Coastlines would be reshaped twice a day. The pull would flex the solid ground as well as the sea.

**\[The breaking point\]**

Here the physics gives a surprising answer. The Moon would never reach the ground in one piece.

Every moon is held together by its own gravity. The closer it gets to a planet, the harder the planet's tides pull its near side and far side apart. There is a distance where that stretching wins. It is called the Roche limit, after the French astronomer Édouard Roche.

For a solid Moon around the Earth, that limit is about nine thousand five hundred kilometres. Inside it, scientists expect the Moon would be pulled apart into pieces.

**\[A ring around the Earth\]**

Those pieces would not fall all at once. They would spread out along the old orbit and form a ring, perhaps like the rings of Saturn. Many of the rings we see around planets lie inside their planet's Roche limit.

Over time, some of the debris would rain down on the Earth. That would be very dangerous for life. But again, this is a thought experiment, not a forecast. Nothing like this is happening to our Moon.

**\[It is happening somewhere\]**

Is there anywhere this is really happening? Yes. Around Mars.

Mars has a small moon called Phobos, which orbits very close to the planet. Unlike our Moon, Phobos is slowly spiralling inward.

In 2015, planetary scientists Benjamin Black and Tushar Mittal, of the University of California, Berkeley, studied what will happen next. They concluded that Phobos is probably too weak to survive. In the next twenty to forty million years, it is expected to crumble and form a ring around Mars. That ring could last between one million and one hundred million years.

So the death of a moon is not only imagination. We can watch the early stages of one, in our own solar system.

**\[A crash in the past\]**

There is one more twist. Most scientists think the Moon itself was born from a crash.

According to the leading explanation, called the giant impact hypothesis, a planet-sized object struck the young Earth about four and a half billion years ago. Scientists often call it Theia. Debris from that collision gathered in orbit and formed the Moon.

The idea fits several clues, such as the Moon's small iron core. But the details are still debated. One puzzle is that Moon rocks are chemically very similar to Earth rocks, which some versions of the theory struggle to explain.

**\[What we still don't know\]**

So what remains open? Scientists are still refining exactly how the Moon formed and how fast its orbit has changed over billions of years. They also keep checking the laser measurements, which are among the longest-running experiments from the Apollo era.

What is not in doubt is the big picture. The Moon is not falling. It is very slowly leaving.

**\[Closing\]**

The Moon began, most likely, with a collision. Phobos shows us how a moon can end. And our own Moon is quietly drifting away, a few centimetres each year, while we measure it with light.

Next time you look up, remember that the Moon is falling, and missing, every single second.
