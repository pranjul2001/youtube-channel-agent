## Script

**\[Hook\]**

On Friday, April 13, 2029, a rock about as wide as three football fields will slip past the Earth. It will come closer than the satellites that carry our television signals. And on a clear, dark night, millions of people will be able to see it without a telescope.

Its name is Apophis. For a few days in 2004, it was the most worrying asteroid ever found. Today, scientists are confident it will miss us. So why are space agencies sending spacecraft to meet it?

This is the story of three dates. A scare, an all-clear, and a visit.

**\[2004: The scare\]**

Apophis was discovered on June 19, 2004, by astronomers Roy Tucker, David Tholen and Fabrizio Bernardi, at Kitt Peak National Observatory in Arizona.

At first, it was just a faint dot with a catalogue number. But when astronomers calculated its path that December, something unusual appeared. The early numbers showed a small chance that it could hit the Earth on April 13, 2029.

As more observations came in, that chance did not shrink. It grew. For a short time around Christmas 2004, it reached about 2.7 percent, roughly one in thirty seven.

That earned Apophis a rating of 4 on the Torino scale, the scale astronomers use to describe impact hazards. No asteroid had ever been rated that high before, and none has been since.

Then astronomers found older images of Apophis, taken months earlier, before anyone knew it was there. With a longer record of its movement, the orbit became much clearer. Within days, an impact in 2029 was ruled out.

**\[Why the worry did not end\]**

But that was not quite the end. Apophis still had a long future to calculate.

When an asteroid passes close to a planet, gravity bends its path. Astronomers worried that, in 2029, Apophis might pass through a tiny region of space called a gravitational keyhole. If it did, Earth's pull could set it on course for an impact years later.

Possible future impact dates were studied, including 2036 and 2068. The 2036 risk was ruled out with better observations. But a very small chance remained for April 2068, around one in three hundred and eighty thousand.

Small, yes. But for an object this size, scientists wanted to be certain.

**\[2021: The all-clear\]**

In March 2021, Apophis made a distant pass by the Earth. Astronomers were ready.

NASA's Goldstone antenna in California sent radar signals toward the asteroid, and the Green Bank Telescope in West Virginia listened for the echoes. Radar measures distance and speed with great precision.

Combined with careful orbit analysis, the result was clear. According to NASA, the uncertainty in Apophis's position for 2029 collapsed from hundreds of kilometres to just a handful.

The 2068 impact was ruled out. NASA's calculations showed no impact risk for at least the next one hundred years. Apophis was removed from both NASA's and the European Space Agency's lists of risky asteroids.

So this part is known, not guessed. Apophis will miss us in 2029.

**\[2029: The visit\]**

Here is what NASA expects on April 13, 2029.

Apophis, about three hundred and forty metres across, will pass less than thirty two thousand kilometres from the Earth's surface. That is closer than satellites in geostationary orbit, which circle about thirty six thousand kilometres up.

NASA says the asteroid should be visible to the naked eye from the Eastern Hemisphere. It will look like a slowly moving point of light, not a fireball. Watchers in Europe, Africa and parts of Asia are best placed.

Close passes like this by an asteroid this large are rare. That is why scientists call it a natural experiment.

**\[What Earth will do to Apophis\]**

Radar suggests that Apophis is shaped a little like a peanut, with two rounded lobes.

As it passes, Earth's gravity will pull on it. NASA says its path will be slightly bent. Its orbit around the Sun will change after the encounter.

What happens to the rock itself is less certain. Scientists believe the flyby could change the way it spins. NASA has even said it could cause small "asteroid quakes," shaking loose dust and boulders on the surface.

These are expectations, not observations. Nobody has watched a large asteroid pass this close to a planet before. That is exactly why two missions are on their way.

**\[The visitors\]**

The first is NASA's OSIRIS-APEX. You may know this spacecraft by its old name, OSIRIS-REx. In 2023, it delivered a sample from the asteroid Bennu back to Earth.

Instead of retiring, it was given a new target. NASA plans for it to reach Apophis shortly after the flyby, around June 2029, and study it for about eighteen months. It will map the surface and its chemistry, and it may even fly close and fire its thrusters to stir up the surface and see what lies beneath. It will not collect a sample this time.

The second is Ramses, a European Space Agency mission built with Japan's space agency, JAXA. ESA approved it in full in November 2025. The plan is to launch in April 2028 and arrive in February 2029, two months before the flyby. If that works, Ramses will be the first spacecraft to ride alongside an asteroid as it passes the Earth, watching every change as it happens.

**\[What we still don't know\]**

So, what is still open?

We do not yet know exactly how Apophis is built inside. Is it one solid rock, or a loose pile of rubble held together by its own weak gravity? The answer matters, because if we ever need to move a dangerous asteroid, its structure would change how we do it.

We do not know how much its spin and surface will change. And the exact figures for its size and distance may still be refined. NASA gives about three hundred and forty metres. ESA gives about three hundred and fifty.

One more thing about the name. Apophis comes from Apep, the serpent of chaos in ancient Egyptian myth. It is a dramatic name for an asteroid that, as far as anyone can calculate, means us no harm.

**\[Closing\]**

In 2004, Apophis was a warning. In 2021, it became a relief. In 2029, it becomes a classroom in the sky, a chance to learn how to protect the Earth from the rocks that really are on their way, somewhere, someday.

If the weather is kind, look up that April evening. You will be seeing a visitor that once had the whole world doing the maths.
