**\[Hook\]**

What caused the largest ship on the Great Lakes to plunge into the dark waters of Lake Superior on a November evening?

You will find out how a fierce storm overpowered a legendary freighter, why her crew of 29 vanished without a distress call, and how the tragedy changed lake navigation forever.

**\[Queen of the Lakes\]**

In 1957, an insurance company contracted a Michigan shipyard to build a massive freighter within a foot of the maximum length allowed for the Saint Lawrence Seaway. GLEW laid the first keel plate on August 7, 1957.

When launched on June 7, 1958, she was 730 feet long, 75 feet wide, and had a 25-foot draft. Her value was $7 million at the time, and she could carry a deadweight capacity of 26,000 long tons.

The ship was named after the board president of Northwestern Mutual, whose family had deep roots in Great Lake shipping. The christening was plagued by misfortunes when the champagne bottle took three attempts to break and a spectator suffered a fatal heart attack.

**\[A Beloved Workhorse\]**

For 17 years, the ship carried taconite iron ore pellets from mines near Duluth, Minnesota, to steel works in Detroit, Toledo, and other Great Lakes ports. She logged an estimated 748 round trips and covered over a million miles.

She set seasonal haul records six different times, earning nicknames like Pride of the American Flag and Mighty Fitz. Her captain was known for playing music over the intercom and chatting with tourists through a bullhorn at the locks.

Despite a few minor groundings and collisions over the years, freshwater ships were built to last over half a century. No one expected her career to end abruptly in the autumn of 1975.

**\[The Final Voyage\]**

On the afternoon of November 9, 1975, the ship embarked from Superior, Wisconsin, carrying a full cargo of taconite ore pellets under the command of Master Captain Ernest M. McSorley. She was bound for a steel mill near Detroit.

She soon reached her full speed of 16.3 miles per hour, traveling alongside another freighter named the Arthur M. Anderson. Weather forecasts initially predicted a storm would simply pass south of Lake Superior by the next morning.

However, a second captain leaving port predicted a major storm would directly cross the lake and took a sheltered north shore route. By midnight, reality caught up as the National Weather Service upgraded warnings for the entire lake.

**\[Into the Winter Storm\]**

As winds and waves intensified, the two freighters sought shelter along the Ontario shore. By the early hours of November 10, the ships encountered a brutal winter storm with plunging temperatures and roaring winds.

Captain McSorley reduced his ship's speed in the rough conditions before pulling ahead of the Arthur M. Anderson. Sustained winds eventually climbed past 50 knots, driving snow that drastically reduced visibility across the water.

By mid-afternoon, the severe environment began exacting a physical toll on the record-setting vessel. The struggle against the lake was turning from difficult to perilous.

**\[Taking on Water\]**

Shortly after 3:30 p.m., Captain McSorley radioed the Arthur M. Anderson to report that his ship was taking on water, had lost two vent covers and a fence railing, and had developed a starboard list. Two bilge pumps ran continuously.

With radar failure striking shortly after 4:10 p.m., the blinded ship slowed down to let the Arthur M. Anderson come within a 10-mile range to provide guidance. McSorley contacted the Coast Guard to ask if navigation beacons were operational.

He told another nearby ship that he was dealing with a bad list and heavy seas in one of the worst conditions he had ever experienced. Experts note the ship was caught in the teeth of a generational gale.

**\[The Last Message\]**

By late afternoon, sustained winds reached nearly 60 knots, and waves grew as high as 25 to 35 feet, accompanied by powerful rogue gusts. The Arthur M. Anderson was battered by the severe meteorological forces.

At approximately 7:10 p.m., the Arthur M. Anderson called to ask how the ship was doing. Captain McSorley gave his final known message: We are holding our own.

No distress signal was transmitted. Ten minutes later, the Arthur M. Anderson could no longer reach the vessel by radio or find her on radar.

**\[The Search Begins\]**

Captain Cooper of the Arthur M. Anderson contacted the Coast Guard at 7:39 p.m. to report the loss of contact. Emergency responders struggled with communication systems because storm antennas had blown down.

A rigorous search was launched into the freezing waters of Lake Superior. On November 14, 1975, a U.S. Navy aircraft detected magnetic anomalies on the lakebed.

The wreck was soon located in Canadian waters about 17 miles from Whitefish Bay. She rested 530 feet deep in two large pieces, her crew of 29 lost to history.

**\[The Unsolved Mystery\]**

To this day, the exact cause of the sinking remains unknown. Countless books, maritime studies, and deep-water expeditions have analyzed the tragedy.

Experts speculate that the ship may have been swamped by massive waves, suffered structural failure, sustained topside damage, or grounded on a shoal. Many believe a combination of these factors doomed the vessel.

What is known is that the tragedy stands as the largest ship to sink on North America's Great Lakes. No human bodies were ever recovered from the site.

**\[Legend and Legacy\]**

The disaster became etched into cultural memory largely through Canadian singer Gordon Lightfoot's popular 1976 ballad. Lightfoot wrote the song after reading a magazine article about the disaster.

Beyond the song, the sinking triggered sweeping changes in Great Lakes shipping regulations. Authorities instituted mandatory survival suits, depth finders, positioning systems, increased freeboard, and more frequent vessel inspections.

The Queen of the Lakes remains a haunting reminder of the unpredictable fury of inland seas. Her memory lives on among boat watchers and historians alike.

**\[Closing\]**

The loss of the SS Edmund Fitzgerald reminds us of the humbling power of nature and the quiet bravery of those who worked the inland seas. Though the exact physical triggers at the bottom of Lake Superior remain a subject of debate, the legacy of her 29 crew members endures.
