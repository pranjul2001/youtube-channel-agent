# The Real Trojan War: Myth, Archaeology, and History

## Script

**\[Hook\]**

A legendary bronze-walled city was burned to the ground around the twelfth century BC, long dismissed as a poet's dream.

For centuries, scholars believed the epic battles of Homer's Iliad were purely mythical inventions.

Then excavations in modern-day Turkey revealed actual ruins bearing the scars of catastrophic fire, reviving one of history's greatest debates.

**\[The Ancient Epic Tradition\]**

The story of the Trojan War reaches us through a web of ancient Greek literature, most famously the epics attributed to Homer. The Iliad focuses on just four days and two nights during the tenth year of a grueling siege. Meanwhile, the Odyssey follows the arduous journey of the hero Odysseus as he tries to return home to Ithaca.

Other lost works, known as the Cyclic Epics, once filled in the rest of the narrative. Poets, tragic dramatists, and later Roman writers like Virgil expanded these tales, cementing them in classical culture. Yet for the longest time, modern scholars viewed the entire conflict as pure fiction without any basis in historical fact.

**\[A Golden Apple and Paris\]**

According to legend, the seeds of the war were planted when Eris, the goddess of discord, tossed a golden apple inscribed with the words "to the fairest" among the goddesses. Hera, Athena, and Aphrodite all claimed the prize. They asked a young Trojan prince named Paris to judge who deserved it.

To win his favor, Aphrodite promised Paris the love of Helen, the most beautiful woman in the world. When Paris traveled to Sparta and took Helen away from her husband, King Menelaus, the stage was set for a massive military retaliation by the unified kingdoms of Greece.

**\[The Gathering of Fleets\]**

Menelaus called upon an ancient oath sworn by all of Helen's former suitors to defend her marriage. His powerful brother, Agamemnon the king of Mycenae, gathered an immense fleet of Achaean troops to sail across the sea. They besieged the massive fortified city of Troy for ten long years.

Ancient sources describe the tragic deaths of legendary warriors on both sides, including the mighty Achilles and Ajax for the Greeks, and the noble Hector and Paris for the Trojans. The conflict finally ended not through brute force alone, but through the famous ruse of the Trojan Horse, allowing hidden Greek soldiers to open the city gates from within.

**\[The Power of Mycenae\]**

To understand the scale behind the legend, archaeologists look to real centers of Bronze Age power like Mycenae in Greece. Located on a high hill overlooking the Argive plain, Mycenae dominated much of southern Greece and the Aegean during the second millennium BC.

The period of Greek history from about 1600 BC to 1100 BC is known as the Mycenaean Age. At its peak around 1350 BC, the citadel and its lower town supported a population of 30,000 people across 32 hectares of land. It was a true military stronghold capable of launching major overseas expeditions.

**\[Discovering the Royal Graves\]**

Inside the ancient citadel of Mycenae, archaeologists discovered extraordinarily wealthy burial sites known as Grave Circle A and Grave Circle B. These royal tombs contained engraved swords, daggers, and golden death masks that proved the existence of affluent warrior chieftains.

Among the discovered items were artifacts like the Mask of Agamemnon and the Silver Siege Rhyton. These objects demonstrated that the civilization described by Homer possessed the immense wealth, craftsmanship, and militaristic culture required to wage a massive war overseas.

**\[Locating Ancient Troy\]**

For centuries, the physical location of Troy remained a mystery, with many doubting it ever existed. By the mid-nineteenth century, both the city and the war were widely considered non-historical myths. That changed in 1868, when German archaeologist Heinrich Schliemann met Frank Calvert.

Calvert convinced Schliemann that the true location of Troy lay at a site called Hisarlık in modern-day Turkey. Excavations began, revealing layer upon layer of ancient cities built on top of one another over thousands of years.

**\[The Evidence of Troy VII\]**

Archaeological excavations at Hisarlık uncovered multiple historical cities, designated by numbers. Many scholars believe that a specific layer, known as Troy VII, corresponds best to the legendary conflict described in ancient epics.

Troy VII shows archaeological evidence of a catastrophic burning and destruction layer that roughly matches the twelfth century BC timeframe. This period also aligns with the broader Late Bronze Age collapse across the Mediterranean region.

**\[History Versus Legend\]**

Today, the exact historicity of the Trojan War remains an open question among scholars. It is known with certainty that Mycenae was a dominant Bronze Age civilization and that Hisarlık contains the ruins of a heavily fortified ancient city destroyed by fire.

Experts generally believe there is a historical core to the tale. However, most historians view Homer's epics as a poetic fusion of various different sieges, trade disputes, and military expeditions carried out by Mycenaean Greeks during the Bronze Age rather than a single literal event.

**\[Closing\]**

What began as an immortal tale of gods, heroes, and a wooden horse ultimately rests upon real foundations of stone, bronze, and ash. Archaeology proved that the world of Homer was not entirely imaginary, even if the divine interventions were pure myth.

As researchers continue to analyze ancient ruins and fragmentary tablets, the line between ancient storytelling and historical reality grows ever more fascinating.
