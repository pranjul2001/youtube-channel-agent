## Script

**\[Hook\]**

Did you know the fastest ocean liner ever to cross the Atlantic was built partly in secret, entirely out of aluminum and fireproof materials, and could carry an entire army division?

We will explore the brilliant mind behind her design, her record-shattering speed trials, and her tragic final voyage decades later.

**\[A Childhood Dream\]**

The legendary ocean liner SS United States was born from a childhood memory. When William Francis Gibbs was just eight years old in 1894, he watched the launch of the SS St. Louis and dreamed of creating a massive, grand American ship that would surpass all others. Decades later, Gibbs became America's foremost naval architect after leading the reconstruction of the massive Leviathan and successfully designing the luxury liner Malolo, which survived a severe collision with a freighter without sinking.

Following World War II, Gibbs finally had the chance to realize his ultimate vision of an American superliner. The US Maritime Commission requested designs for a vessel that could operate as a luxury passenger liner during peacetime and quickly transform into a troop transport during war. Gibbs submitted his winning design, setting the stage for an engineering marvel.

**\[Military Roots\]**

During wartime, governments frequently seized large ocean liners to move soldiers across oceans. The newly formed US Department of Defense wanted a ship specifically engineered for this rapid troop-deployment role. If mobilized, the ship's onboard furnishings could be completely removed to accommodate a 14,400-man US Army division.

Because of her immense size and incredible speed, experts calculate she could rapidly deploy a full military division anywhere in the world without needing to stop for fuel. Her design was a direct collaboration between civilian operators and the United States Navy, incorporating rugged military-style engineering beneath her elegant exterior.

**\[Building the Leviathan's Successor\]**

The ship's construction began in a dry dock on February 8, 1950, after an aircraft carrier project in the exact same dry dock was canceled. This made her the first ocean liner to be built in a dry dock, which allowed workers to prefabricate parts and accelerate construction. During her build, the Department of Defense briefly requisitioned the one-third-completed vessel due to a shortage of troop transports during the Korean War, though the decision was reversed a month later.

The total cost reached $71.08 million, heavily subsidized by the US government to ensure national defense features were built right into her frame. She was finally christened and launched on June 23, 1951, sponsored by Lucile Connally. She was owned by United States Lines and stood ready to claim the seas.

**\[Power and Propellers\]**

The ship relied on a powerhouse engineering plant developed in close cooperation with the Navy, featuring isolated engineering spaces and extensive redundancies. Eight boilers generated massive amounts of steam to turn four Westinghouse double-reduction geared turbines. Each turbine generated approximately 60,000 shaft horsepower, feeding four drive shafts.

The propulsion system hid a clever secret in its propellers. The two outboard propellers had four blades, while the two inboard propellers had five blades. Each propeller measured 18 feet in diameter and was specially built to rotate efficiently in either direction, minimizing vibrations and helping the ship achieve staggering velocities.

**\[Aluminum Towers\]**

William Gibbs knew that tall funnels could dangerously compromise a ship's stability, a lesson learned from the older Leviathan. To keep the vessel from becoming top-heavy, Gibbs made the massive funnels and the entire superstructure out of lightweight aluminum. At the time, this was the world's largest aluminum construction project and the first major application of aluminum on a ship.

The teardrop-shaped funnels stood 55 feet tall and 60 feet wide, holding the record as the largest funnels ever put to sea. Fabricating them required immense care to prevent galvanic corrosion where the aluminum met the steel decking, creating a striking red-white-and-blue silhouette that defined the ship's iconic look.

**\[Shattering Records\]**

The maximum speed of the SS United States has long been a subject of dispute, once guarded closely as a military secret. While rumors and leaked reports claimed she hit up to 43 knots during early runs, official records show her top achieved speed reached 38.32 knots during a full-power trial run on June 10, 1952.

This blistering pace easily earned her the Blue Riband for the highest average speed across the Atlantic Ocean in either direction. That prestigious title remains uncontested to this day, cementing her place in maritime history as the fastest ocean liner ever built.

**\[Fireproof Interiors\]**

To prevent the horrific fires that plagued earlier liners like the Morro Castle and Normandie, William Gibbs enforced strict fire safety rules. Wood was almost entirely banned from the ship, save for a single wooden butcher's block in the galley. Furniture, fabrics, and fittings were custom-made from glass, metal, and fiberglass to meet rigorous Navy fireproofing standards.

Even the grand ballroom's piano, originally designed in aluminum, was made from mahogany only after a dramatic demonstration where gasoline was poured onto the wood and ignited without catching fire. Asbestos-laden paneling was widely used throughout the interior, and exterior decks were covered with a green latex composition called Neotex instead of traditional teak.

**\[Class Segregation\]**

Following classical tradition, Gibbs incorporated a strict three-tiered class system for passengers. The ship featured separate dining rooms, bars, public spaces, and recreation areas for first-class, cabin-class, and tourist-class travelers. Passengers were largely expected to keep to their own designated areas, only intermingling in shared spaces like the gymnasium, swimming pool, and theater.

At maximum capacity, the ship carried 894 first-class, 524 cabin-class, and 554 tourist-class passengers. First-class tickets started around $350 during a standard season, granting access to the ship's most luxurious facilities, including the grand ballroom, smoking room, and the famous Duck Suite popularized by the Duke and Duchess of Windsor.

**\[Art and Elegance\]**

Interior designers Dorothy Marckwald and Anne Urquhart sought to create a fresh, contemporary look emphasizing simplicity and restrained elegance over traditional glitz. To capture the spirit of America, artists Hildreth Meière and Austin Purves decorated the ship with hundreds of unique art pieces themed around aspects of the country, such as the Mississippi River and native fauna.

Many of these sculptures, relief murals, and paintings incorporated aluminum to match the ship's sleek black-and-silver theme while remaining lightweight and fireproof. Nearly 200 aluminum sculptures lined the first-class stairway alone, showcasing state bird and flower emblems on every deck landing.

**\[Retirement and Tows\]**

Despite her record-breaking speeds and popularity among celebrities and immigrants, passenger counts dropped sharply in the mid-1960s with the rise of jet-propelled trans-Atlantic flights. In 1969, a surprise announcement pulled the ship from service, canceling all planned cruises. She changed hands repeatedly over the following decades as various owners tried and failed to make her profitable while she sat aging and poorly maintained.

Her interior furnishings were sold at auction in 1984, and the rest of her interiors were stripped down to the bulkheads by 1994. In 1996, she was towed to Philadelphia, where she remained docked for nearly three decades.

**\[The Final Voyage\]**

In 2009, the SS United States Conservancy began raising funds to save the historic liner, eventually purchasing her in 2011 with hopes of restoration. However, a rent dispute led to her eviction from her Philadelphia pier in 2024. With no other viable locations found to dock her, Okaloosa County in Florida purchased the ship with a unique final purpose.

On November 11, 2026, the legendary liner was deliberately sunk near Destin to become an artificial reef. This final voyage surpassed the USS Oriskany as the world's largest artificial reef and made the SS United States the largest passenger liner ever to sink.

**\[Closing\]**

The SS United States went from a child's dream of engineering supremacy to a record-breaking maritime titan, and finally to a sunken monument beneath the waves. She remains an unmatched symbol of mid-century American ambition and engineering ingenuity.
