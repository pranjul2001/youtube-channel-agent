**\[Hook\]**

How does a monument guarded every single second of the day end up with an empty crypt beneath its marble floor?

While millions visit Arlington National Cemetery to witness the precision of the U.S. Army's Old Guard, the history behind the site involves hidden struggles, heavy stone, and profound human stories.

This story explores the selection of the unknown service members, the construction of the iconic tomb, and the strict traditions of the sentinels who watch over them.

**\[The First Unknown\]**

Following the devastation of World War I, nations sought a way to honor those whose bodies could never be identified. On Memorial Day in 1921, four unknown servicemen were exhumed from different American cemeteries in France.

U.S. Army Sergeant Edward F. Younger, who had been wounded in combat and decorated for valor, made the final choice in a city hall in Châlons-en-Champagne, France, on October 24, 1921.

Younger walked around four identical caskets and placed a spray of white roses on the second casket from the right. That unknown soldier was transported to America aboard the USS Olympia and laid to rest on November 11, 1921.

**\[Carving the Yule Marble\]**

For a decade, the initial grave rested beneath a simpler marker while plans for a grand superstructure moved forward. Congress authorized $50,000 for the final design by architect Lorimer Rich and sculptor Thomas Hudson Jones.

Workers spent a year quarrying Yule marble from a site 3.9 miles south of Marble, Colorado. Separating the massive middle block from the mountain required 75 men and a wire saw to trim a 124-ton raw piece down to 56 tons.

After a four-day journey by rail and processing by craftsmen in Vermont, the marble blocks arrived at Arlington. The completed monument was finished without a formal ceremony on April 9, 1932.

**\[Symbols on the Stone\]**

The finished tomb features meticulous carvings across its four main levels. The large middle block, or die, features detailed panels facing all four directions.

The north and south panels originally represented a world of memories before coming to symbolize six major battles fought by American forces in France: Ardennes, Belleau Wood, Château-Thierry, Meuse-Argonne, Oisur-Eisur, and Somme. Each wreath carved into these panels contains 38 leaves and 12 berries.

The east panel facing Washington, D.C., features three Greek figures representing peace, victory, and valor. The west panel bears the famous inscription: Here Rests in Honored Glory an American Soldier Known but to God.

**\[World War II and Korea\]**

Decades later, President Dwight D. Eisenhower signed legislation to honor the unknown service members of subsequent global conflicts. In 1958, selection ceremonies took place for World War II and the Korean War.

The World War II unknown was chosen from remains gathered across Europe, Africa, Hawaii, and the Philippines, while the Korean unknown came from the National Cemetery of the Pacific. Enlisted service members made the final selections aboard naval vessels.

Both men were transported to Washington, where they lay in state in the Capitol Rotunda before President Eisenhower awarded each the Medal of Honor at Arlington National Cemetery.

**\[The Vietnam Crypt\]**

A fourth crypt was added to the plaza for an unknown service member from the Vietnam War. Marine Corps Sergeant Major Allan Jay Kellogg Jr. designated the unknown during a ceremony at Pearl Harbor, and the remains eventually rested at Arlington after traveling across the country.

Years later, investigative reporting and advancements in science changed the course of this monument. In 1994, activist Ted Sampley gathered evidence suggesting the remains belonged to Air Force First Lieutenant Michael Joseph Blassie, who was shot down in Vietnam in 1972.

Following a CBS News broadcast and mounting political pressure, the body was exhumed in May 1998. Mitochondrial DNA testing confirmed the remains were indeed those of Lieutenant Blassie, and he was returned home to his family in St. Louis.

**\[A Vacant Space\]**

With the identification and removal of Lieutenant Blassie's remains, the Vietnam crypt was left without an occupant. Authorities chose not to place another unknown service member in the space.

Instead, the stone slab above the crypt was replaced. The new inscription reads Honoring and Keeping Faith with America's Missing Servicemen 1958 to 1975.

This change serves as a permanent reminder of the armed forces' ongoing commitment to the fullest possible accounting of all missing personnel.

**\[The Old Guard\]**

Protecting the sacred site requires constant vigilance from the United States Army's 3rd Infantry Regiment, known as The Old Guard. Soldiers stationed at nearby Fort Myer volunteer for the grueling assignment.

Fewer than 20 percent of all applicants are accepted into training, and only a fraction of those candidates successfully earn the right to serve as full sentinels. The Tomb Guard Identification Badge is currently the third least-awarded qualification badge in the entire U.S. Army.

The soldiers walking the mat wear no rank insignia on their uniforms. This deliberate choice ensures they never outrank the unknown service members resting beneath the stone, regardless of what military rank those individuals held in life.

**\[Walking the Mat\]**

The tomb is guarded continuously around the clock, through every type of weather. Sentinels execute precise, calculated steps across the black rubber mat, turning and pausing at exact intervals.

An elaborate changing of the guard ceremony takes place every half-hour from April through September, and hourly from October through March.

Sentinels are authorized to maintain strict decorum around the memorial. If visitors cross physical barriers or behave disrespectfully, the guards will immediately issue verbal reprimands to preserve the solemn atmosphere.

**\[Closing\]**

The Tomb of the Unknown Soldier stands as a testament to sacrifice, holding the memories of those who served without recognition.

Behind the polished marble and the rhythmic footsteps of the guards lies a deeply human story of remembrance, identity, and national devotion.
