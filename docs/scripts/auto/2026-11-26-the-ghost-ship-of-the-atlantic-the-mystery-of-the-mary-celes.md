# The Ghost Ship of the Atlantic: The Mystery of the Mary Celeste

Series: Unsolved Mysteries. Written automatically on 2026-11-26 from the sources below; 1646 words. Review before publishing.

## Script

**\[Hook\]**

A sturdy merchant vessel drifted entirely deserted in the Atlantic Ocean, its sails half-set and its cargo completely untouched.

Found on December 4, 1872, the American-registered brigantine Mary Celeste held ample provisions and the personal belongings of its crew, yet not a single soul remained on board.

What drove experienced sailors to abandon a seaworthy ship in the open ocean, and why were they never seen again? We will examine the recorded facts of the discovery, the fierce courtroom investigations, and the enduring speculations behind maritime history's most famous ghost ship.

**\[From Amazon to Mary Celeste\]**

The ship that would become an enduring mystery began its life in Canada. Built of locally felled timber at Spencers Island in Nova Scotia, the vessel was launched on May 18, 1861, under the name Amazon. It was rigged as a brigantine with two masts and measured 99.3 feet in length, 25.5 feet in breadth, and 11.7 feet in depth, with a gross tonnage of 198.42.

The ship's early career was plagued by unfortunate mishaps. During its maiden voyage to London, its first captain, Robert McLellan, fell ill and died on June 19, 1861. Under subsequent commanders, the Amazon collided with fishing equipment off Maine and accidentally sank another brig in the English Channel. In October 1867, the ship was driven ashore in a storm at Cape Breton Island and abandoned as a wreck.

An American mariner named Richard W. Haines acquired the wrecked hull, repaired it, and registered it in New York under a new name: Mary Celeste. Following financial seizures and changes in ownership, the ship underwent a major refitting in early 1872 that cost $10,000. Workers enlarged the vessel to a length of 103 feet and a displacement of 282.28 tonnes, adding a second deck and extending the poop deck before Captain Benjamin Spooner Briggs took command.

**\[A Careful Voyage Begins\]**

Captain Benjamin Briggs was a deeply religious man from Massachusetts and an experienced mariner who invested his savings into a share of the Mary Celeste. For her first voyage following the extensive refitting, the ship was scheduled to carry 1,701 barrels of alcohol from New York City to Genoa, Italy. Briggs chose to bring his wife, Sarah, and their infant daughter, Sophia, along with him on the journey.

Briggs carefully selected a competent crew to accompany them. First mate Albert G. Richardson had sailed under Briggs before, and second Andrew Gilling was joined by steward Edward William Head and four German seamen from the Frisian Islands: Volkert and Boz Lorenzen, Arian Martens, and Gottlieb Goudschaal. Before departure, Briggs wrote to his mother that the vessel was in beautiful trim.

On November 5, 1872, the Mary Celeste left Pier 50 in New York Harbor. Due to uncertain weather, Briggs anchored near Staten Island for two days before finally heading out into the Atlantic. By coincidence, a Canadian brigantine named Dei Gratia, captained by David Morehouse, lay nearby in Hoboken loading petroleum for Genoa and set sail along the same general route eight days later.

**\[Encounter on the Ocean\]**

On December 4, 1872, the Dei Gratia reached a position midway between the Azores and Portugal. Around 1 pm, Captain Morehouse's helmsman spotted a vessel drifting unsteadily toward them at a distance of about six miles. The erratic movements and peculiar set of sails raised immediate suspicion.

Finding no one on deck and receiving no response to signals, Morehouse sent first mate Oliver Deveau and second mate John Wright in a small boat to investigate. Upon climbing aboard, the men confirmed the ship was the Mary Celeste. The vessel was in a dishevelled but seaworthy condition under partial sail, with damaged rigging and ropes hanging loosely over the sides.

While the main hatch cover was secure, the fore and lazarette hatches were open with their covers resting on the deck. The ship's single small lifeboat was missing, and the binnacle housing the compass had shifted with its glass cover broken. About three and a half feet of water rested in the hold—a significant amount, but not alarming for a ship of that size—and an abandoned makeshift sounding rod lay nearby.

**\[Inside the Abandoned Ship\]**

Deveau entered the cabin area and found the daily logbook, with its final entry dated at 8 am on November 25, nine days earlier, recording the ship's position near Santa Maria Island in the Azores. The cabin interiors were damp and untidy from water entering through doorways and skylights, but otherwise orderly.

Personal items belonging to Captain Briggs and his family were scattered around, including a sheathed sword resting under a bed. However, most of the ship's official papers and navigational instruments were completely missing. There were no obvious signs of fire or violent struggle, leading observers to conclude that the crew had made an orderly departure using the missing lifeboat.

Experts and relatives later debated why anyone would willingly leave a sturdy vessel for a small boat. Some speculated the crew feared an imminent explosion from the alcohol cargo or structural failure, while others argued that abandoning a sound ship for an open boat defied basic maritime logic. Whatever the motive, none of the people on board were ever seen or heard from again.

**\[The Journey to Gibraltar\]**

Recognizing a potential salvage claim under maritime law, Captain Morehouse decided to bring the derelict vessel into Gibraltar, located 600 nautical miles away. Because the Dei Gratia was already operating with a small crew of eight, Morehouse split the men, sending Deveau and two experienced seamen to manage the Mary Celeste while he and four others remained on the Dei Gratia.

Sailing two seriously undermanned ships through winter weather proved difficult and slow. The Dei Gratia arrived in Gibraltar on December 12, 1872, and the Mary Celeste followed the next day after encountering heavy fog. The vice admiralty court immediately impounded the ship to prepare for official salvage hearings.

Deveau noted in a letter to his wife that the ordeal of navigating the derelict to port was exhausting, but expressed confidence that they would be well paid for the rescue. Little did the salvors know, the arrival in Gibraltar would trigger an aggressive legal examination driven by suspicion.

**\[Suspicion in the Courtroom\]**

The salvage hearings began on December 17, 1872, under Sir James Cochrane, the chief justice of Gibraltar. The case was prosecuted by Frederick Solly-Flood, the Attorney General of Gibraltar, whose rigid personality convinced him early on that a violent crime must have taken place.

Flood ordered an inspection of the ship by surveyor John Austin and a diver named Ricardo Portunato. Austin reported finding cuts on each side of the bow, which he attributed to a sharp instrument, alongside possible traces of blood on the captain's sword. He also emphasized that a vial of sewing machine oil remained upright, citing it as proof the ship had not experienced heavy weather, though he did not consider whether the vial could have been placed there after the abandonment.

Further inspections by Royal Navy captains endorsed the belief that the bow cuts were deliberate and reported finding stains on one of the ship's rails. These suspicious details formed the basis for theories involving mutiny, piracy, or insurance fraud.

**\[Foul Play Unproven\]**

Despite Flood's persistent suspicions of murder, mutiny, or a conspiracy between the crews of the Mary Celeste and the Dei Gratia, no convincing evidence ever supported those dark theories. Subsequent chemical analysis suggested the sword stains were not blood, and the cuts on the hull were likely caused by normal wear against floating debris or docking equipment.

The court ultimately found no proof of foul play, piracy, or intentional fraud. However, the presiding officers harbored lingering, unresolved doubts that affected the final outcome of the legal proceedings.

Because of these lingering suspicions, the court awarded a relatively low salvage payout to Captain Morehouse and his crew. The inconclusive nature of the hearings left the door wide open for decades of public speculation.

**\[Myths and Fantasies\]**

Following the Gibraltar hearings, the true story became heavily obscured by false details, tall tales, and complete fabrications. Over the years, sensational writers introduced dramatic myths, such as steaming-hot meals left on cabin tables and steaming coffee cups—none of which appeared in the official records.

A wide variety of imaginative hypotheses emerged to explain the desertion, ranging from waterspouts, submarine earthquakes, and giant squid attacks to paranormal intervention. One scientific theory suggested that fumes from the cargo of 1,701 barrels of alcohol built up in the hold and frightened the crew into temporarily fleeing the ship in the small boat.

As the ship passed through subsequent owners, it met an unglamorous end. In 1885, its final captain deliberately wrecked the vessel off the coast of Haiti as part of an insurance fraud scheme, bringing a physical close to the ship's history.

**\[The Enduring Legend\]**

Though the Mary Celeste met its demise off Haiti, its legend grew larger in death than in life. Dramatized in plays, films, and documentaries, the ship's name permanently entered the English language as a universal byword for an inexplicable, eerie desertion.

In 1884, author Arthur Conan Doyle published a fictional short story titled 'J. Habakuk Jephson's Statement' based loosely on the mystery. The immense popularity of Doyle's story cemented a common spelling error, causing the public to frequently refer to the vessel as the 'Marie Celeste'.

More than 150 years later, the exact sequence of events that caused Captain Briggs, his family, and crew to leave a sound ship remains unknown. The verified historical record preserves the facts of the discovery, while the ultimate fate of the occupants stays lost to the Atlantic.

**\[Closing\]**

The mystery of the Mary Celeste endures because it touches on our deep fascination with the unknown. We are left with a seaworthy vessel, an intact cargo, and an empty horizon where a family and crew once stood.

While investigators searched for villains and conspiracies, the true answer was likely a quiet, mundane maritime emergency that escalated beyond recovery. Some historical questions are destined to remain open, reminding us of the vast and unpredictable nature of the sea.
