# The Unread Book: History's Most Mysterious Coded Manuscript

## Script

**\[Hook\]**

Inside a climate-controlled vault in Yale University rests a hand-written book from the 15th century that no living person can read.

For hundreds of years, brilliant cryptographers, linguists, and military codebreakers have tried to unlock its secrets, only to walk away empty-handed.

What is this bizarre illustrated volume, and why has its unknown script resisted every single attempt at decryption by modern science?

**\[A Rare Discovery\]**

The manuscript is a hand-written, illustrated codex consisting of around 240 vellum pages bound together in uneven sections. Its pages feature folding sheets of varying sizes and are covered in fantastical illustrations, strange diagrams, crude coloring, unidentified plants, and astrological symbols.

The book was first confirmed in the 17th century in the possession of Georg Baresch, an alchemist living in Prague. Centuries later, in 1912, a Polish book dealer named Wilfrid Voynich purchased the volume, giving it the name by which it is known today. Since 1969, it has been housed in Yale University's Beinecke Rare Book and Manuscript Library.

**\[Vellum and Ink\]**

In 2009, researchers sent tiny samples of the manuscript to the University of Arizona for radiocarbon dating. The tests returned consistent results pointing to a creation date between 1404 and 1438. Later protein testing revealed that the parchment was crafted entirely from calfskin, using at least 14 or 15 entire animal skins.

Microscopic analysis shows that a quill pen and iron gall ink were used for the text and figure outlines. The ink contains major amounts of carbon, iron, sulphur, potassium, and calcium. The pages also feature colored paints—including blue azurite, red-brown ochre, and green copper compounds—applied somewhat crudely to the ink outlines.

**\[Worn and Used\]**

Medievalist Lisa Fagin Davis describes the parchment as unusually soft, a texture typically found in books that have been heavily handled and thumbed through. This heavy usage strongly indicates that the manuscript served a routine, workmanlike role, such as a medical manual or celestial almanac, rather than sitting on a shelf as a sacred or ceremonial object.

The physical pages show natural flaws like holes and tears from insect bites or wounds, and the book lacks luxurious touches like gold leaf. Insect holes on the first and last folios suggest that a wooden cover protected the volume before later leather bindings were added.

**\[The Strange Script\]**

The book contains roughly 38,000 words written from left to right in an entirely unknown script, often referred to as Voynichese. Most of the characters are made of one or two simple pen strokes, and a script of 20 to 25 distinct characters accounts for virtually all of the text. There is no obvious punctuation to guide the reader.

The handwriting flows smoothly across the pages, giving the impression that the symbols were written fluently rather than encoded slowly through a rigid cryptographic cipher. While the vast majority of the text remains unreadable, a few stray bits of Latin script appear in the margins, including month names and a few words in High German and distorted Latin.

**\[Stumping the Experts\]**

Over the decades, professional and amateur cryptographers have tackled the text without success. During both World War I and World War II, elite American and British codebreakers attempted to read the manuscript. Experts like William Friedman, Elizebeth Friedman, Prescott Currier, and John Tiltman all tried and failed.

In 1962, Elizebeth Friedman described statistical analyses of the book as doomed to utter frustration. Because words that differ by only single letters repeat with unusual frequency, simple substitution methods consistently yield absolute babble.

**\[Natural Language Clues\]**

Despite its illegibility, statistical studies suggest the text shares deep structural traits with real human languages. The word frequencies follow Zipf's law, meaning the most common word appears roughly twice as often as the second-most-common word. Furthermore, the ratio of unique words to total words closely mirrors languages found around the world.

In 2014, a team led by Diego Amancio used statistical methods to analyze word relationships and concluded that in 90% of cases, the system resembles actual books rather than random gibberish. Linguists Claire Bowern and Luke Lindemann also noted that while character sequences are more predictable than standard languages, the manuscript's higher-level organization matches natural language properties rather than standard cipher codes.

**\[Distinct Sections\]**

The manuscript is divided into distinct sections based on its vivid illustrations, covering plants, stars, bathing women, and pharmaceutical charts. Interestingly, topic modeling reveals that different sections use different sets of overrepresented words, exactly as one would expect in a normal book with chapters dedicated to different subjects.

Linguist analyses also suggest the writer may have used two distinct encoding methods across different parts of the book. One style, known as Voynich A, appears mainly in the herbal and pharmaceutical pages, while Voynich B appears in the astrological and balneological sections.

**\[Countless Theories\]**

Scholars currently lack the historical context and translation key needed to prove any specific origin or purpose. Hypotheses regarding the book's creation run the gamut from a natural language or constructed script to an intricate cipher, a medieval hoax, or a folkloric reference compendium.

Other experts have suggested the text might be glossolalia—meaningful only to a writer experiencing ecstatic speech—or even an early work of science fiction, fantasy, or mythopoeia. To this day, none of these proposed hypotheses have been independently verified.

**\[Online Access\]**

For over half a century, the fragile artifact remained securely locked away in Yale's rare book library, accessible only to a select group of approved scholars and researchers who traveled to view it in person.

That changed in 2020, when Yale University published high-resolution digital photographs of every single page online. This digital release opened the mysterious codex to a global community of amateur sleuths and curious minds.

**\[Closing\]**

Whether the Voynich Manuscript is a lost natural language, an elaborate 15th-century hoax, or an encrypted medical guide, it remains one of history's most compelling puzzles.

Until someone finally cracks its unread pages, the little book of Yale will keep its silent secrets.
