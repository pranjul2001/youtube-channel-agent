**\[Hook\]**

Can a casual chat at a roadside diner over coffee spark a multi-trillion dollar revolution in modern computing?

What started as a bold gamble on video game graphics has quietly transformed into the invisible engine driving artificial intelligence across the globe.

**\[The Denny's Meeting\]**

In April 1993, three engineers gathered in a roadside diner to sketch out an ambitious vision for the future of computing. They shared a frustration with their current employers and a belief that processing three-dimensional graphics could solve massive computational problems that general-purpose chips could not. Armed with $40,000 in the bank, they launched their startup from a townhouse in Fremont, California, eventually choosing a name derived from the Latin word for envy.

During the late 1990s, roughly 70 startup companies pursued the exact same idea that graphics acceleration was the definitive path forward. Only two of those companies survived the intense market competition. Yet even among survivors, early survival was far from guaranteed when their initial product design relied on quadrilateral primitives instead of the triangle primitives preferred by industry standards.

**\[A Near Collapse\]**

The company's very first graphics accelerator failed to gain traction because Microsoft chose to support only triangles for its DirectX platform. At the same time, a major contract with Sega for a video game console fell through when Sega decided to work with a different hardware vendor. With funds dwindling rapidly, the company was left with just enough money for one month of payroll.

In a pivotal moment of survival, Sega's president visited in person and persuaded his management to provide $5 million in funding, giving the struggling startup six months to live. The co-founder and chief executive officer responded by laying off more than half of the staff, reducing the headcount from 100 down to 40, and pivoting entirely to a new chip optimized for triangle rendering.

**\[Gaming As Fuel\]**

Video games served as the ultimate testing ground because they were simultaneously one of the most computationally challenging problems and possessed incredibly high sales volume. This combination provided a financial flywheel to fund massive research and development into heavy data processing. By late 1999, the company released the GeForce 256, widely considered the first product expressly marketed as a graphics processing unit, which outperformed existing hardware by a wide margin.

Success in consumer graphics soon led to a contract to develop hardware for Microsoft's Xbox game console, along with the acquisition of rival pioneer 3dfx. As these graphics processors grew more capable, experts realized their unique ability to handle massively parallel computations could be harnessed for tasks far beyond rendering virtual worlds.

**\[The Parallel Power\]**

In the early-to-mid 2000s, the company invested over a billion dollars to develop a software platform called CUDA, which enabled graphics processors to run parallel programs for compute-intensive applications. This platform created a self-reinforcing cycle by attracting a growing community of developers who utilized the technology for scientific research and general-purpose computing. The number of developers using this software platform climbed from 1 million in 2018 to 2 million in 2021, and ultimately reached 5 million by 2024.

Experts note that this software ecosystem fundamentally changed how computers approached heavy workloads. What began as a dedicated circuit for drawing pixels on a monitor evolved into a flexible processor capable of tackling molecular simulation, complex data science, and industrial applications.

**\[The AI Explosion\]**

By 2025, Nvidia controlled more than 80% of the market for processors used in training and deploying artificial intelligence models, while also powering more than 75% of the world's top supercomputers. Demand for data center hardware soared to unprecedented heights during the artificial intelligence boom, driving the company's market capitalization past historic milestones. In 2025, it became the first company in the world to surpass both US$4 trillion and US$5 trillion in market value.

The physical components driving this modern technological shift became so valuable that transport logistics required extraordinary security measures. Industry executives noted that specialized hardware shipments destined for massive data centers frequently arrived at their locations transported directly by armored car.

**\[Corporate Turmoil\]**

As the company achieved dominant status on stock market indexes, it also navigated complex organizational changes and high-profile leadership transitions. By 2024, half of the firm's employees earned over $228,000, cementing its reputation as a highly sought-after employer in Silicon Valley during a broader tech industry downturn.

Later, in November 2026, co-founder Jensen Huang resigned from his roles as chief executive officer, president, and board member following reports regarding personal conduct. Following this sudden management change, the board of directors established a new leadership position titled chair of the board to guide the company forward.

**\[Closing\]**

The evolution of graphics processing units demonstrates how a specialized tool designed to render video games can eventually reshape the entire landscape of human technology. What began as a sketched idea over coffee at a roadside diner now powers the foundational architecture of modern artificial intelligence.

As hardware demands continue to climb alongside our computational ambitions, the silicon chips resting inside our computers remain a testament to the power of a well-timed pivot.
