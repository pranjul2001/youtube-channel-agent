# How a Garage Startup Became the Everything Store

## Script

**\[Hook\]**

Could a simple legal mishearing change the course of retail history forever? Let us find out how a modest online bookstore grew into a global economic titan.

We will explore the logistics, strategies, and key moments that built the modern digital marketplace.

**\[The Regret Minimization Framework\]**

In 1994, Jeff Bezos walked away from his position as a vice president at a Wall Street firm named D. E. Shaw & Co. He decided to move to Seattle, Washington, driven by what he termed a regret minimization framework. He wanted to ensure that when he grew old, he would not regret missing out on the early days of the internet.

Working out of a rented home's garage on Northeast 28th Street in Bellevue, Washington, Bezos and programmer Shel Kaphan began drafting a business plan. Initially, the enterprise was incorporated in Washington state on July 5, 1994, under the name Cadabra, Inc. That name did not last long.

A lawyer accidentally misheard the original title as cadaver. Seeking a fresh start, Bezos consulted a dictionary and settled upon Amazon, named after the world's biggest river because he intended to build the biggest bookstore in the world. He also appreciated that starting with the letter A would place his company at the very top of alphabetized lists.

**\[Selling Books Online\]**

After reading a report predicting that annual web commerce would grow by 2,300%, Bezos compiled a list of 20 potential products to sell online. He narrowed this down to five promising items: compact discs, computer hardware, computer software, videos, and books. Books ultimately won out because of massive worldwide demand, low unit prices, and millions of titles in print.

Bezos's parents invested nearly $245,573 in the startup. On July 16, 1995, the online bookseller officially opened its digital doors. The very first item purchased by a customer was Douglas Hofstadter's book titled Fluid Concepts and Creative Analogies: Computer Models of the Fundamental Mechanisms of Thought.

Sales grew at a startling pace. Within the first two months, orders shipped to all 50 states and over 45 countries, pushing weekly revenue up to $20,000. Barnes & Noble soon filed a lawsuit alleging that calling itself the world's largest bookstore was false because Amazon operated as a book broker. The dispute eventually settled out of court, and Amazon kept using the slogan.

**\[Expanding the Catalog\]**

By June 1998, the company moved past literature by adding music to its online storefront. Walmart soon filed its own lawsuit in October 1998, accusing Amazon of stealing trade secrets by hiring former Walmart executives. That case also settled out of court, though it prompted Amazon to implement internal restrictions and reassign those employees.

A distinct curved arrow leading from the letter A to the letter Z was introduced into the corporate logotype between October 1999 and February 2000. Shaped like a smile, the design signaled that the company intended to sell every product imaginable, earning it the popular moniker of the everything store.

Skeptics doubted whether the business model could ever turn a profit. During the dot-com bubble crash of 2001, many e-commerce competitors vanished entirely. Yet Amazon persevered, finally posting its very first quarterly profit of 1 cent per share in late 2001 on revenues exceeding $1 billion.

**\[Scaling Up and Stumbling\]**

Employment numbers climbed dramatically as the operation expanded. In 2011, the company maintained 30,000 full-time employees in the United States. By the end of 2016, that workforce had surged to 180,000 employees globally.

Not every product launch succeeded. In 2014, Amazon introduced the Fire Phone to handle media streaming, but poor consumer demand resulted in a $170 million loss. Production on the device halted the following year.

In August 2014, Amazon successfully acquired the social video gaming streaming platform Twitch for $970 million, integrating it into its broader entertainment and production divisions.

**\[Physical Stores and Groceries\]**

Seeking to challenge traditional brick-and-mortar retail giants, Amazon announced plans in June 2017 to acquire Whole Foods Market, a high-end supermarket chain with over 400 stores, for $13.4 billion. Shareholders and federal regulators approved the deal by August 2017.

The company also experimented with automated physical retail. In January 2018, Amazon Go opened to the general public in Seattle after an initial testing period for employees. The store used cameras and sensors to track items shoppers grabbed from shelves, automatically charging their digital accounts and eliminating checkout lines entirely.

Physical retail experiments shifted over time. In September 2018, the company debuted Amazon 4-star stores, which sold highly rated items from its website. However, by March 2022, Amazon decided to close all of its 4-star, books, and pop-up locations in the US and UK to focus more heavily on grocery and fashion offerings.

**\[HQ2 and Pandemic Surges\]**

In September 2016, Amazon announced a competition for a second major headquarters, known as HQ2, which promised up to 50,000 jobs and a $5 billion investment. After an extensive bidding process, the company selected New York City and Arlington County, Virginia, in November 2018. By February 2019, Amazon canceled the Queens location following local opposition, choosing to focus entirely on the Crystal City neighborhood of Arlington.

When the COVID-19 pandemic struck in early 2020, online shopping surged dramatically. To handle the overwhelming demand for household staples, Amazon hired approximately 175,000 additional warehouse workers and delivery contractors while temporarily raising wages by $2 per hour.

The massive influx of orders also brought workplace tensions. At the end of March 2020, workers at a Staten Island warehouse staged a walkout to protest health conditions during the pandemic, leading to the firing of organizer Chris Smalls.

**\[Acquisitions and Entertainment\]**

Entertainment holdings expanded significantly in May 2021 when Amazon entered negotiations to acquire Metro-Goldwyn-Mayer due to the studio's financial struggles during the pandemic. The merger deal was finalized in March 2022 for a total value of $8.45 billion.

This acquisition brought the historic MGM film and television library into the Amazon Prime Video catalog. By October 2023, MGM Holdings fully merged into Amazon Studios, which was subsequently renamed Amazon MGM Studios.

The company also invested heavily in artificial intelligence. In September 2023, Amazon announced a strategic partnership with AI startup Anthropic, committing an investment of up to $4 billion while making Amazon Web Services the startup's primary cloud provider.

**\[Logistics and Warehousing\]**

Delivering billions of packages required vast logistical networks. In September 2018, Amazon launched its last-mile delivery program by ordering 20,000 Mercedes-Benz Sprinter Vans to handle regional shipping independently.

By 2020, Amazon generated $386 billion in US retail e-commerce sales, marking a 38% increase over the previous year. Marketplace sales by third-party vendors formed an increasingly dominant share of this revenue.

With more than one million workers employed in warehouses around the world by 2023, Amazon began testing humanoid robots to handle partial automation tasks, such as positioning empty boxes and tracking inventory storage locations.

**\[Corporate Adjustments\]**

Rapid expansion eventually gave way to corporate restructuring. On November 14, 2022, Amazon announced plans to lay off 10,000 corporate and technology employees.

That workforce reduction target increased to 18,000 employees in a January 2023 announcement. In March 2023, the company revealed it would eliminate an additional 9,000 jobs.

Despite corporate cutbacks, the company's influence over modern retail and cloud computing remained deeply entrenched across global markets.

**\[Closing\]**

From a single garage in Bellevue to a global network of servers, automated warehouses, and streaming studios, Amazon redefined how the world shops and connects.

What began as an ambitious bet on books ultimately reshaped the modern economic landscape.
