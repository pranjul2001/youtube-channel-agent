## Script

**\[Hook\]**

On the night of December 15, 1900, the crew of a passing steamer looked toward a lonely Scottish lighthouse and saw something wrong. The light was out. Eleven days later, when a relief ship finally reached the island, the three men who were meant to keep that light burning were gone. No bodies. No note. No clear answer, even today.

In this video I will separate what we know, what experts believe, and what storytellers added later.

**\[The place\]**

The Flannan Isles are a tiny group of rocky islands in the Outer Hebrides, roughly twenty miles west of the Isle of Lewis, in the open Atlantic. The largest is Eilean Mor. On it stands a lighthouse designed by David Alan Stevenson, first lit in December 1899. In December 1900, it had been working for a little over a year.

The station was run by three keepers on the island, while a fourth man rested ashore on rotation. It was lonely, difficult work, and the lamp had to be lit every night.

**\[The men\]**

The three on duty were James Ducat, the principal keeper. Thomas Marshall, an assistant keeper. And Donald MacArthur, an occasional keeper filling in for William Ross, who was on sick leave.

These were ordinary working men with lives ashore. Ducat left a wife and four children, and MacArthur a wife and two children.

**\[The light goes out\]**

On the night of December 15, the steamer Archtor passed the island and the crew saw no light. Accounts differ on whether that sighting ever reached the Northern Lighthouse Board in time to matter.

But the relief ship, the Hesperus, was held back by bad weather. It did not reach the island until noon on December 26.

The captain sounded the ship's horn and fired a rocket. No one answered. So relief keeper Joseph Moore was sent ashore in a boat.

He found the lighthouse deserted. The clock had stopped. The kitchen was clean, the lamps were trimmed and ready, and the doors were closed. Of Ducat, Marshall and MacArthur, there was no sign anywhere on the island.

That day, the captain sent a telegram to the Board saying that a dreadful accident had happened.

**\[The investigation\]**

On December 29, Robert Muirhead, a superintendent of the Northern Lighthouse Board, arrived to investigate. He knew the men personally. He had recruited all three. His report is dated January 8, 1901, and it is still the main official account.

He noticed some important details. Ducat and Marshall had gone out in their sea boots and waterproofs, the gear the men wore when going down to the landings. But MacArthur's coat was still in the lighthouse. He seems to have gone out in his shirt sleeves, which suggests he left in a hurry.

There was also fresh storm damage near the west landing: railings displaced and twisted.

**\[The leading explanation\]**

Muirhead's conclusion was simple, and it is still the leading explanation. He believed the men had been on duty up to dinner time on Saturday the 15th, and then went down to secure a box where mooring ropes were kept. A large wave, which he called a roller, swept them into the sea.

A later theory adds one more detail: that MacArthur saw the danger, ran down to warn or help the others, and was lost with them. That part is a theory, not part of the official finding.

No one saw it happen. This is an informed judgment, not a proven fact. Even the weather is debated: there were no reported storms in the area on the days before, yet the west landing was clearly damaged.

**\[What is legend\]**

Now for what is not true. You may have heard of a logbook with strange entries: terrible storms, men praying, a keeper behaving oddly. Those entries were not part of the original record. They were added by storytellers years later.

The story kept growing. A famous 1912 poem, "Flannan Isle," by Wilfrid Wilson Gibson, made the mystery even more haunting. A 2018 film, The Vanishing, was based on the case. Drama like this keeps the story alive, but it is not evidence.

**\[What remains unknown\]**

There are real unanswered questions. We do not know exactly when they disappeared, or in what order. We do not know why MacArthur left without his coat. And we will probably never know exactly what the sea took from the island that day.

**\[Closing\]**

The Flannan Isles mystery is not a story of ghosts or sea monsters. It is the story of three men doing a dangerous job at the edge of the world, and of an ocean that gave no answers. A memorial to the three keepers has since been placed on Lewis, so they are remembered by name.

### Changed from v1 and why
- Ages removed (43/40/28): the NRS archivist's records imply Ducat was 44 and Marshall about 31; only Discovery UK gives 43/40/28.
- Marshall's role is "assistant keeper", not "second assistant" (NRS records).
- Archtor/Leith line: W says the sighting reached the Board at Leith on 18 Dec, but NRS says it was not communicated. The script now states the dispute.
- Removed "no fire in the grate" (not in any readable source) and "beds were empty" (sources say unmade, or as normally left).
- Oilskins: Muirhead says Ducat and Marshall wore their boots and waterproofs. The v1 wording "missing oilskins" is replaced with what he actually wrote.
- "MacArthur followed": this is James Love's later theory, not Muirhead's finding, so it is now labelled as a theory.
- "Most historians favor": no source says this, so it now reads "still the leading explanation".
- Calm seas at the Hesperus arrival came from one local booklet and echoes the fake logbook ("sea calm"). Replaced with the documented point: no reported storms, yet damage at the landing.
- Removed the line about "voices calling the keepers' names": no source.
- h2g2 could not be read (HTTP 403), so it is dropped as a source. The Oban Times memorial report is dated Dec 2017, not 2018.
